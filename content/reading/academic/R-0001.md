# Living Roofs

**A** Look down on almost any large city from a tall building and you will see a landscape of grey: flat concrete roofs, gravel, metal vents and the occasional water tank. For most of the twentieth century, the roof was treated as a purely practical surface whose only job was to keep the rain out. In recent decades, however, architects and city planners have begun to see these neglected spaces differently. A growing number of buildings are now topped with soil and plants, creating what are known as green roofs or living roofs. Supporters argue that these small patches of vegetation can help cities deal with some of their most stubborn environmental problems.

**B** The idea itself is far from new. In parts of northern Europe, farmhouses were covered with layers of turf for hundreds of years, mainly because grass and soil provided excellent insulation during long winters. What distinguishes the modern green roof is its engineering. A typical system today consists of several distinct layers: a waterproof membrane that protects the building, a barrier that stops roots from pushing through, a drainage layer that allows excess water to escape, a lightweight growing medium, and finally the plants themselves. Each layer must be carefully designed, because a leak in a roof covered with soil is much harder to find and repair than a leak in an ordinary one.

**C** Green roofs are usually divided into two broad categories. Extensive roofs have a thin growing layer, often less than fifteen centimetres deep, and are planted with hardy, low-growing species such as mosses, grasses and sedums, which can survive long periods without water. They are relatively light, need little maintenance and are generally not intended for people to walk on. Intensive roofs, by contrast, have much deeper soil and can support shrubs, vegetable beds and even small trees. They often function as roof gardens or parks, but they are heavy, expensive to install and require regular watering and care, so the building beneath must be strong enough to carry the extra weight.

**D** One of the main reasons cities encourage green roofs is their effect on rainwater. When heavy rain falls on hard surfaces, the water runs off almost immediately into drains, and in older cities this sudden flow can overwhelm the sewer system and cause flooding. A planted roof behaves more like a sponge. The soil and plants absorb a large share of the rain and release it slowly, either through evaporation or by allowing it to drain away gradually over several hours. Engineers point out that even a modest delay in run-off can make a significant difference during a storm, because it spreads the load on the drainage network over a longer period.

**E** Living roofs may also help to cool cities. Urban areas are often noticeably warmer than the surrounding countryside, partly because dark surfaces such as asphalt and roofing materials absorb heat during the day and release it at night. Plants reduce this effect in two ways: they shade the surface beneath them, and they release moisture into the air, which has a cooling effect. Inside the building, the extra layer of soil acts as insulation, keeping upper floors cooler in summer and reducing the heat that escapes in winter. Researchers at a university in Scandinavia who monitored a group of office buildings found that the top floors of those with green roofs needed less air conditioning in hot weather than similar buildings with conventional roofs.

**F** There are ecological benefits as well. A well-planned green roof can provide food and shelter for insects such as bees and butterflies, as well as for some species of birds. Some designers deliberately leave areas of bare gravel or dead wood to attract particular creatures. It would be a mistake, however, to imagine that a rooftop can replace a natural habitat. Roofs are exposed to strong winds and intense sunlight, and they are often isolated from other green spaces, so the range of species that can live there is limited. Ecologists generally see green roofs as stepping stones that help wildlife move across a city rather than as complete habitats in themselves.

**G** Despite these advantages, green roofs remain the exception rather than the rule. The most obvious obstacle is cost: installing a living roof is considerably more expensive than fitting a conventional one, and while owners may save money on energy over time, the savings can take many years to cover the initial investment. Older buildings may also need structural reinforcement before they can carry the weight of wet soil. For these reasons, several cities have introduced incentives, such as grants or reduced fees, for owners who install green roofs, and a few now require them on certain types of new development. Whether such policies spread more widely may depend on how convincingly the long-term benefits can be demonstrated.

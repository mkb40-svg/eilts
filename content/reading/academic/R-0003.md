# Finding Our Own Way

**A** Not long ago, travelling to an unfamiliar place required a certain amount of preparation. People studied paper maps before setting off, wrote down lists of street names, and asked strangers for directions when they inevitably went wrong. Today, most of us simply type an address into a phone and follow the voice that tells us when to turn. The change has happened so quickly that it is easy to forget how recent it is. In little more than a generation, a skill that humans relied on for most of their history has become, for many people, almost unnecessary.

**B** It would be foolish to pretend that this development has not brought real benefits. Satellite navigation has made travel less stressful for millions of people, particularly those who are anxious about driving in unfamiliar areas. Delivery drivers can reach more addresses in a day, emergency services can locate callers more precisely, and tourists can explore foreign cities without fear of getting hopelessly lost. I have no wish to return to the days of arguing with a passenger over a badly folded road map. Yet I believe that we have given up something of value in exchange for this convenience, and that we have done so without fully considering the cost.

**C** The skill in question is what psychologists call spatial navigation: the ability to build a mental picture of our surroundings and to work out where we are in relation to other places. When we navigate without help, we notice landmarks, estimate distances and keep track of the direction we are facing. Following turn-by-turn instructions requires none of this. We can arrive at a destination having paid almost no attention to the route, and many people find that they are unable to repeat a journey without the device that guided them the first time. Researchers who asked volunteers to walk through an unfamiliar town, some using a phone and others a paper map, reported that the map users later drew far more accurate sketches of the area. The finding will not surprise anyone who has followed a phone across a city and remembered nothing of it afterwards.

**D** Some scientists go further and suggest that relying on navigation devices may affect the brain itself. There is evidence that the areas of the brain involved in spatial memory become more active when people plan their own routes, and some researchers have speculated that, like a muscle, these areas may weaken if they are rarely used. I am cautious about such claims. The human brain is remarkably adaptable, and it is quite possible that any changes of this kind could be reversed with practice. The research in this field is still at an early stage, and it is a long way from showing that a person's brain has changed to proving that this change matters in daily life. Popular articles often present tentative findings as if they were established facts, and this does little to help the public understand the real state of knowledge.

**E** Even if the effects on the brain turn out to be small, I would argue that there are other reasons to value the ability to find our own way. Navigating without assistance forces us to engage with the place we are in: to look at buildings, notice the slope of a street, and connect one neighbourhood to another in our minds. A city learned in this way becomes familiar in a way that a city experienced through a screen never quite does. Visitors who explore on foot often come across markets, cafes and quiet squares that they would never have found by following the quickest route. There is also the simple matter of independence. Batteries run out, signals fail in tunnels and remote valleys, and a person who has never practised reading a map may find themselves genuinely helpless when the technology lets them down.

**F** None of this means that we should abandon our devices. What I am suggesting is a more deliberate approach to using them. We might, for example, study a route on a map before setting off and use the phone only if we become confused, or switch off the voice instructions on journeys that we make regularly. Parents could encourage children to lead the way on family walks, and schools could do more to teach basic map reading, which is often treated as an old-fashioned subject with little relevance to modern life. Such small changes would allow us to keep the convenience of modern technology while protecting a skill that has served our species well for a very long time. Getting lost occasionally, it turns out, may be a reasonable price to pay for knowing where we are.
